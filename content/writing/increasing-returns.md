> [thesis|thesis]
> Strategy is not position on a board. It is movement inside an ecology.

## Source

> “You need an awareness of the ecology you are in. If you think of different firms and products as being different species, then you have to be very aware of how that entire network of different companies operates, even if they are quite peripheral to you.”
>
> — W. Brian Arthur, *Increasing Returns and the New World of Business* (Harvard Business Review, 1996)

## Frame

This is the part of strategy people keep flattening.

They look for the company, the product, the moat, the feature, the market. But increasing returns do not operate on isolated objects. They operate on networks: complements, standards, habits, switching costs, distribution paths, developer mindshare, regulation, taste, and timing.

The unit is not the firm.

The unit is the ecology.

## Claim

A product compounds when the environment starts making the product more valuable than the product can make itself.

That is what increasing returns feel like from the inside. Not linear execution. Not “we shipped more.” A loop begins to form:

- more users create more use cases
- more use cases attract more builders
- more builders create more complements
- more complements make the core more valuable
- the core becomes easier to choose because the world around it is already shaped for it

At that point, strategy becomes less like choosing a position and more like steering a habitat.

## Operator

The useful question is not only:

> What are we building?

It is:

> What world becomes more likely if this works?

Then map the ecology:

| Layer | Question |
|---|---|
| Users | Whose behavior changes if this gets adopted? |
| Builders | Who can create value on top of it? |
| Complements | What adjacent tools, data, standards, or habits make it stronger? |
| Constraints | What bottleneck stops the loop from closing? |
| Weak ties | Which peripheral actor can unexpectedly reshape the system? |
| Feedback | What gets easier because it already happened once? |

The weak ties matter because ecosystems often move through the side door. The player that looks peripheral today may control the future interface, the standard, the dataset, the trust layer, or the distribution path.

## AI version

AI makes this ecological view mandatory.

A model is not a product by itself. It becomes a product inside a system of prompts, workflows, evals, tools, memory, data rights, integrations, habits, and trust boundaries.

A demo shows capability.

An ecology shows compounding.

That is why evals, traces, reruns, and workflow design matter. They are not process overhead. They are the substrate where a one-time capability becomes a reusable loop.

## Test

A strategy is weak if it can only explain why the product is good.

A strategy is stronger if it can explain what loops form around the product, who else gets pulled in, and which constraints become more valuable once the system starts moving.

The map should include the obvious actors.

The advantage is usually in the actors that still look peripheral.
