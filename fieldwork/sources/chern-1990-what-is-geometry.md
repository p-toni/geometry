# source — Chern 1990: What Is Geometry?

Status: **stressed**  
Provenance: Shiing-Shen Chern, “What Is Geometry?”, *The American Mathematical Monthly* 97(8), 1990, 679–686. DOI: 10.2307/2324574.  
Branch: `mathematical-geometry-native`

## Native question

Rather than define geometry once and for all, what historical developments changed what mathematicians could treat geometrically?

## Claims in the source's own terms

Chern explicitly refuses to give a fixed definition and instead presents a historical sequence of enlargements:

1. axioms and deduction — Euclid;
2. coordinates — Descartes and Fermat;
3. calculus — Newton and Leibniz;
4. transformation groups and invariants — Klein and Lie;
5. manifolds and local geometry — Riemann, with topology globalizing local neighborhoods;
6. fiber bundles and connections — Cartan and Whitney.

The Klein stage characterizes a geometry by properties invariant under a transformation group, but Chern does not present that as the final essence of geometry. Riemannian geometry localizes geometry; topology patches local neighborhoods into a global manifold; connections describe transport between local fibers; curvature can generate global topological invariants.

His conclusion is deliberately loose: contemporary geometry is far from Euclid, coordinates on a manifold need not have intrinsic meaning, and the story is incomplete and will continue to expand.

## Evidence / argument actually offered

This is a historical-conceptual essay by a major geometer, illustrated with canonical mathematical developments: non-Euclidean geometry, analytic curves, the Erlangen program, Riemannian metrics, manifolds, Gauss–Bonnet, characteristic classes, gauge theory, and an application to DNA topology.

It is not a proof that all of these developments share one hidden essence.

## Explicit limits / unknowns

- Chern calls the story “one-sided and incomplete” and explicitly labels it his personal viewpoint.
- He does not provide necessary and sufficient conditions for something to count as geometry.
- The six-step list is historical, not a theorem of conceptual dependence.
- Group invariance is one major stage, not a claim that every later geometry reduces to the Erlangen program.

## Assumptions under pressure

The word `geometry` can survive substantial changes in method, object, and formal apparatus. Historical continuity of a field does not imply one invariant definition underneath it.

## Rival reading

One could still argue that the sequence admits a deeper unification—relations, transformations, locality, invariance, or structural equivalence—but Chern's essay itself does not establish which unification is correct. Reading one back into the history would be our inference.

## Possible displacement of the seed

**Candidate displacement:** our current thesis gives invariants and symmetries a privileged early position. Native mathematical geometry is broader. Invariance under groups is a powerful organizing idea, but local structure, global gluing, transport, connection, and curvature become equally central later.

If `geometry` remains our word, we may eventually need to say which of those operations we actually mean rather than treating “navigable structure” as sufficient by itself.
