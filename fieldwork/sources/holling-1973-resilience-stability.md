# source — Holling 1973: resilience and stability of ecological systems

Status: **stressed**  
Provenance: C. S. Holling, “Resilience and Stability of Ecological Systems,” *Annual Review of Ecology and Systematics* 4, 1973, 1–23. DOI: 10.1146/annurev.es.04.110173.000245. Reprint: https://pure.iiasa.ac.at/26/1/RP-73-003.pdf  
Branch: `ecological-resilience`

## Native question

Should ecological persistence be judged by constancy and return to equilibrium, or by the ability of relationships to persist despite large disturbances and state changes?

## Claims in the source's own terms

Holling distinguishes a stability-oriented view concerned with fluctuations around an equilibrium from a resilience-oriented view concerned with persistence in systems exposed to unexpected change. For variable ecological systems, constancy can be less informative than whether the relationships maintaining the system continue to exist.

Later summaries of this tradition distinguish ecological resilience from return-time notions of engineering resilience and connect resilience to the amount of disturbance tolerated before a system shifts to another regime.

## Evidence / argument actually offered

The paper synthesizes ecological models and empirical behavior to argue that the two perspectives answer different questions and have different management consequences.

## Explicit limits / unknowns

- Ecology does not supply one universal definition of resilience.
- “Persistence of relationships” can be difficult to operationalize and is level-dependent.
- Resilience of an undesirable regime is still resilience; the concept is not a value judgment.

## Assumptions under pressure

Persistence need not mean return to the same point or maintenance of low variance. A system can move substantially and still preserve the organization that matters.

## Rival reading

For engineered systems operating near a designed setpoint, return-time stability may be the appropriate measure. Ecological resilience should not be generalized into a universal replacement for stability.

## Possible displacement of the seed

**Candidate displacement:** distinguish state stability from regime/relationship persistence. “What survives?” may concern a basin, organization, or set of relations rather than a particular state or output.
